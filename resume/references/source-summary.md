## Central argument: two audiences

Resumes still matter. They must work at two stages:

1. **Hiring software:** systems read, rank, and organize applications, so the resume must be parseable and show a clear match to the role.
2. **Human review:** recruiters need credible evidence, specific contributions, and readable language to move an application forward.

Rules 1–2 below address software readability and fit; rules 3–5 address human judgment. The source reports that only 6% of hiring managers describe AI as the decision maker for passing or rejecting applicants. This is the author's reason for emphasizing both audiences, not evidence that every employer uses the same workflow.

## Rule 1: make the resume readable

- Optimize for readability rather than decorative appearance. The author contrasts this with visual templates promoted by AI design tools.
- Use one column and conventional headings such as Summary, Experience, Education, and Skills. Avoid skill bars, heavy graphics, decorative icons, and large images.
- Export a PDF with selectable text. Test by highlighting and copying text; inability to do so may indicate image-only content.
- Aim for a file below 2.5 MB because some parsers reportedly reject larger files. The transcript explicitly says there is **no universal file-size limit**; follow the actual employer/system requirement.
- Consider local application norms. The author gives anecdotes about headshots and zodiac-sign requests in China. These are contextual anecdotes, not a universal requirement or a reason to collect unnecessary personal information.

## Rule 2: make the fit obvious

- **Keyword mapping** uses relevant job-description terms to describe work the candidate actually did. **Keyword stuffing** crams in repeated or unsupported phrases.
- Give the assistant both the job description and the base resume. First identify the employer's problems, then extract important skills and keywords, and only then propose stronger bullets.
- Review every suggestion and keep only terms backed by a real, explainable experience. Remove unsupported terms rather than maximizing coverage.
- The source contrasts a repetitive customer-support bullet with an illustrative, concrete result: reducing customer complaints by 31% using AI to turn help-centre documents into an onboarding email sequence. This is an example, not the user's achievement.

## Rule 3: use AI for clarity and preserve the person's thinking

- AI can help with spelling, grammar, wording, and concision. Generic prompts and identical polished phrases can obscure the candidate's distinctive qualifications.
- Avoid vague language such as “results-driven professional” and inflated statements about cross-functional excellence when the underlying work is not explained.
- First capture rough, even messy, notes for each experience or project covering **the final result, what the candidate specifically contributed, and how they achieved it**. Facts come before polish.
- Then supply the job description and those notes, and ask for stronger bullet wording **without changing any underlying facts**.
- Keep only language the candidate can explain naturally in an interview.
- In the source's refund-guide example, the candidate compared conflicting sales/support replies and wrote a shared guide. The polished example highlights that reduction and the shared guide; it must not become a claim about the user.
