# Resume

Build a resume that is easy for hiring software to read and gives human reviewers concrete evidence of fit. Preserve the user's facts and voice. Reply in the user's requested language; do not infer the resume's language or hiring market from file paths.

## Preview before changing the resume

The user's standing preference is to review alternatives before edits are applied.

- At the beginning of a rewrite response, show the original bullet and three proposed versions: **A. Clear and direct**, **B. Impact-focused**, and **C. Formal and measured**. Keep all three grounded in the same facts. When there are several bullets, group the alternatives by experience/project and identify each bullet.
- Prefer **result → personal contribution → how it was achieved** in all three styles. Tone may differ; factual strength, ownership, and evidence must not increase across versions.
- After the options, briefly flag missing evidence, useful clarifying questions, and any meaningful tradeoff. Recommend an option only with a reason. Do not choose for the user.
- Wait for the user's selection before replacing resume text or editing an existing resume file. Apply approval only to the selected wording and scope; then show the final text or file. The user may explicitly change this preference or authorize a broader batch.
- Analysis and clearly labelled proposals are allowed before selection. Creating or updating this skill is separate from approving changes to a resume. If no resume facts exist yet, ask for them instead of inventing personal alternatives.

## Gather facts and establish fit

Read the base resume, rough notes, and job description before asking questions. Accept messy notes. If the user is unsure how to start, ask for an existing resume or a short account of their experience and the role they want; do not require a long questionnaire.

For every work experience or project, establish:

1. **Final result:** what changed, who benefited, and the verified measure, baseline, timeframe, or deliverable.
2. **Personal contribution:** what the user specifically did, distinguishing their work from the team's result.
3. **Method:** the actual actions, tools, decisions, collaboration, and checks that produced the result.

Ask focused questions where a missing fact affects the wording. Keep unknowns outside proposed submission-ready bullets; label incomplete work as a draft. If no outcome or metric is known, describe a confirmed deliverable without inventing impact and explain what evidence would strengthen it.

With a job description, first identify the employer's problems and priority requirements. Map each relevant skill or keyword to an actual experience or project, then draft alternatives. Use the employer's terminology only where the evidence supports it. Flag genuine gaps; do not hide them with repeated keywords or unsupported skills. Without a job description, use the stated role direction and identify the limits of that tailoring.

## Write evidence-based bullets

- Use the user's requested sequence: **result → what I contributed → how I achieved it**. With verified metrics, use XYZ: **Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z**. Combine these into a natural sentence rather than adding three artificial labels to every bullet.
- Strengthen verbs and make wording concise without upgrading responsibility. Do not turn “assisted” into “led,” group work into sole ownership, a prototype into a deployed system, an intended benefit into an achieved result, or association into proven causation.
- Preserve names, dates, tools, qualifications, scope, units, numbers, baselines, timeframes, and the meaning of comparisons. Never invent achievements, causal claims, certifications, or results.
- Suggest relevant measures beyond revenue: time saved, speed, scale, volume, accuracy, quality, errors, cost, customer outcomes, or adoption. Ask for verifiable figures; estimates must be confirmed and labelled. Retain original figures by default. Show any proposed derived percentage with its calculation before using it; distinguish percentages from percentage points.
- Use AI for clarity, spelling, grammar, and structure while retaining the user's specific decisions and natural voice. Remove generic claims such as “results-driven professional” and unsupported grand verbs. Keep language the user can explain naturally in an interview.
- Use relevant keywords in meaningful statements, not lists of synonyms. Do not optimize toward a keyword-coverage target or invent ATS/interview scores.
- Translate without changing factual meaning or asserting unverified equivalence between countries' degrees, certifications, and titles.

## Demonstrate relevant AI skills

When AI experience matters to the target role, show the problem, how and why the user actually used AI, their own contribution and checks, and the result. A tool name in the skills section alone is weak evidence.

If a genuine AI achievement is among the most relevant results, propose putting it first within that experience. Do not force an AI bullet into every job or displace stronger relevant evidence just to mention AI. Do not infer AI use from automation alone.

If the user lacks a workplace example, propose a small role-relevant project and describe what to build. List it as completed only after the user has actually done it; use a separate Projects section. Evidence may include inspectable work samples or outcomes, an interview/task demonstration, and completed courses or certifications, with each described accurately.

Where available, use a direct link to the user's existing portfolio, repository, or project document. A document can explain the situation, why and how AI was used, validation, and what changed. Do not fabricate links, publish work, or disclose employer/customer data as part of resume editing.

## Make the resume readable and deliver it

Default to one column, selectable text, conventional headings such as Summary, Experience, Projects, Education, and Skills, and consistent dates. Avoid skill bars, decorative graphics, large images, and information encoded only in icons. Use only sections that help the application.

Respect the user's layout choices and actual application requirements. Ask about the target market if it materially affects the format; do not turn the transcript's anecdote about photos or zodiac signs into a request for personal details. Usually put relevant experience before education for experienced applicants; for new graduates, choose the order that foregrounds the strongest evidence. Do not impose a universal one-page rule.

Follow the employer's required file type and upload limit. Otherwise prefer a PDF containing selectable text, ideally below 2.5 MB; that size is a heuristic from the source, not a universal ATS limit. For requested Word/PDF production, use available document/PDF skills as needed, inspect actual layout, and check text extraction and reading order, links, and file size. A copy/paste check is a useful minimum, not a guarantee of compatibility with every system.

After wording selection, deliver the approved content or requested file with a short change note and unresolved factual questions. Check all final statements against the source material, personal versus team ownership, dates, metrics, job relevance, interview-defensible wording, and remaining placeholders. Do not claim validation that was not performed. Resume preparation does not authorize application submission or contact with recruiters.
